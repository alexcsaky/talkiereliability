# Talkie reliability backtest: experiment plan

Nov 19, 2026 · @Alexander Csaky

## Summary

This is a plan for a backtest of one claim behind reliability tracking: that a forecaster told the track record of its sources forecasts better. The forecaster is Talkie, a 13B language model trained only on text from before 1931, asked about 1931 to 1940. Status: plan drafted by Claude on 19 Nov 2026 from six research agents' reports; nothing is built; no decision in it is Alex's except those listed next.

Decided by Alex on 19 Nov: the aim is a rigorous backtest, not only a showpiece; staging is open-ended; the build is a new standalone repo; compute is chosen from the research; and a modern "librarian" model makes tool calls for Talkie so that the test is of Talkie's reasoning and not its tool use.

The design in brief:

- About 1,500 questions about the 1930s, generated by rule from 1930 lists so that hindsight does not pick them, each answered by Talkie through token probabilities.
- An archive of pre-1931 newspapers, speeches and periodicals, searched lexically.
- A ledger giving each of 60 to 150 sources a track record from forecasts that resolved by 1930.
- Arms that differ only in the track records attached to identical passages: none, real, shuffled and inverted. The main result is real against shuffled.
- Track records enter three ways: in the prompt, in passage selection, and as weights applied in code.

Three things the research changed:

1. **Talkie has leaked knowledge of the 1930s and 1940s**, by its authors' account. Every question gets a leak probe, the famous events are kept as a separate stratum, and the confirmatory run uses the base model, since the chat model was tuned with modern Claude models.
2. **Models often ignore credibility labels in a prompt.** So the plan does not rest on Talkie reading a score: weights applied outside the model are tested alongside, and a synthetic task checks whether Talkie can use a score at all.
3. **The first gate needs no vintage model.** Before Talkie forecasts anything, the ledger is tested on whether a source's record up to 1920 predicts its accuracy in 1926 to 1930. That is a direct test of reliability tracking, and Cowles's 1933 result suggests it may fail for market forecasters.

Expected compute cost is $30 to $180 for the full run on a rented GPU, plus an unestimated modern-model cost for the ledger. The coding agent's brief is a separate file, `talkie-backtest-build-brief.md`.

## What the research found

Six research agents reported on 19 Nov 2026. The experiment appears to be new, and two findings changed the design: Talkie is not fully out of sample for the 1930s, and models tend to ignore credibility labels placed in a prompt. Labels: verified means an agent read it in the primary source; reported means a secondary source; hypothesis means an agent's or Claude's inference. Claude has not re-read the sources.

### Talkie

- Three official checkpoints, all Apache 2.0, last changed 23 Apr 2026: the 1930 base model, the 1930 chat model and a modern web twin. No hosted API; the official demo is chat only (verified, [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/talkie-lm), [repo](https://github.com/talkie-lm/talkie)).
- It is custom code, not a standard architecture, with no log-probability call. Community ports to transformers exist and report a close match to the reference (verified, model cards).
- **It leaks.** The authors say the 13B model knows some details of the Second World War, the United Nations and the division of Germany, from misdated documents and later editorial insertions (verified, [Talkie post](https://talkie-lm.com/introducing-talkie)). Users report it giving the year as 1939 (reported, repo issues). This falls in the target decade.
- The chat model was post-trained with Claude Sonnet 4.6 as judge and on chats with Claude Opus 4.6 (verified, Talkie post), so it is not a clean 1930 mind.
- The training corpus is not released (verified).
- Its only forecasting measurement is how surprising about 5,000 New York Times "On This Day" descriptions are to the model, by decade. No questions, probabilities or retrieval (verified).
- One reviewer of the forecasting field calls Talkie too weak to draw conclusions from for long-range forecasting (verified, [Astral Codex Ten](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-ai-forecasting-agenda)). A separate paper measured Talkie at 34.5 per cent strict recall of events before its own cutoff (verified, [TypewriterLM paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02991)).

### Prior work

- No study was found that gives a forecaster, vintage or modern, track-record scores for its sources and measures the effect. No scored forecast of the 1930s by any vintage model was found; tests since April are informal chats, such as Talkie rating a great war in the near future as very unlikely (verified, [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47927903)).
- Credibility labels in a prompt gave results from an 18-point gain to a 14-point loss depending on the model, and the authors conclude that models are not inherently sensitive to them (verified, [Pan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06809)). Weights applied outside the model have a better record: performance weights beat equal weights out of sample in 26 of 33 expert-judgement studies (verified from the abstract, [Cooke's classical model review](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/reensy/v163y2017icp109-120.html)).
- Backtests leak in ways that are easy to miss: an audit of date-filtered search found major leakage on 71 per cent of questions (verified, [arXiv 2602.00758](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00758)), and questions generated after the fact skew towards things that happened (verified, [Paleka et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00723)).
- Reliability tracking itself has design sketches but no test that scores predict later accuracy (verified, [Forethought](https://forethought.org/research/design-sketches-collective-epistemics)).
- Scoring forecasters is older than the cutoff: Cowles scored financial services and publications in 1933 and found no skill (verified), and Cox appraised six business forecasting services in 1929 (reported).

### What would be new

1. A scored, probabilistic forecast test of a vintage model on events after its cutoff.
2. A retrieval test on a vintage model.
3. A causal test, real against placebo, of whether source track records improve forecasts.
4. A test of whether reliability scores predict later accuracy at all.

Items 3 and 4 bear directly on the reliability tracker. Item 4 does not depend on Talkie.

## Hypotheses

The claim under test: a forecaster that is told the track record of its sources forecasts better than one that reads the same sources without it. Everything below is a proposal until the plan is frozen at the end of stage 1.

1. **H1, the main effect.** Given identical retrieved passages, Talkie's forecasts of 1931 to 1940 questions score better when each passage carries its source's real track record than when it carries none.
2. **H2, specificity.** Real track records beat shuffled ones. If shuffled scores help as much as real ones, the effect is formatting, not information.
3. **H3, the model is using them.** Inverted track records (best sources labelled worst) make forecasts worse than no track records.
4. **H4, dose.** The benefit is larger on questions where the retrieved sources disagree and their track records differ most.
5. **H5, the signal exists at all.** A reliability-weighted vote over the sources' own positions, computed in code with no language model reading the scores, beats an unweighted vote.

Secondary questions, reported but not confirmatory: whether the archive helps over closed book, and whether the interactive librarian mode behaves differently from fixed evidence.

### How each outcome would be read

| Outcome | Reading |
| --- | --- |
| Real beats none and real beats shuffled | Supports the claim: track-record information improved forecasts |
| Real and shuffled both beat none, equally | A formatting or attention effect; no support |
| H5 holds but H1 does not | The signal is in the ledger but this model cannot use it in a prompt. Argues for applying reliability outside the model (ranking, filtering, weighting) |
| Nothing differs, H5 also flat | Track records from before 1931 carried no usable signal for the 1930s on these questions. A real negative result for this setting |
| Real is worse than none | Over-trust in stale records. Also a real finding, and a warning for the product |
| Inverted scores change nothing | The manipulation failed; no conclusion about the claim can be drawn from H1 |

## Design

Every question is answered under every arm, and the evidence for a question is retrieved once and cached, so the arms that differ in track records read identical passages. Anything that leaks through retrieval is then present in both sides of the main comparison.

The only thing that changes between arms is the step in colour: which track records, if any, are attached to the cached passages.

### Arms

| Arm | What Talkie is given | What it tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Closed book | The question only | Baseline and leak probe |
| 1. Archive | Four to six pre-1931 passages from a fixed panel of sources, each with source name and date | Whether evidence helps at all |
| 2. Real records | The same passages, each with its source's real track record | H1 |
| 3. Shuffled records | The same passages, track records permuted across sources | H2 |
| 4. Inverted records | The same passages, track-record ranks reversed | H3 |
| 5. Anonymous sources | Arms 1 and 2 again with source names masked | Whether Talkie's own opinions of named sources drive the result |
| Ceiling | `talkie-web-13b-base`, closed book | What a model that knows the answers scores on this format |

### Three ways a track record can enter

All three are run for arms 2 to 4, because prior work finds that models respond inconsistently to credibility labels in a prompt (verified, Pan et al. 2024).

- **A, in the prompt.** Talkie reads a period-style line beside each passage, at three levels, for example "Of twenty forecasts by this journal examined, fourteen were borne out." This is the test of Talkie's own reasoning with the information.
- **B, in the selection.** Track records decide which passages are shown or their order. Talkie never sees a score.
- **C, in the pooling.** Talkie forecasts from each passage separately and the code weights those forecasts by track record. This reuses the arm 1 forward passes, so it costs nothing extra.

### Two run modes

- **Fixed evidence (confirmatory).** A scripted pipeline: lexical search, cached passages, base model. All hypothesis tests come from this mode.
- **Interactive librarian (exploratory).** Talkie's chat model asks questions in plain language and a modern model makes the tool calls for it under the constraints in the librarian section. This shows how Talkie reasons with the tools, feeds the demo, and is reported separately.

### Controls that run before the main arms

- **Synthetic task.** Invented entities with two conflicting sources, where only the track record identifies the right answer. If mode A fails here, a null from mode A on real questions cannot be interpreted.
- **Manipulation check.** Swap the track records on fixed passages and record how far the log-odds move.

## Forecaster

The confirmatory run uses the base model, `talkie-1930-13b-base`, read through token probabilities. The chat model is exploratory only, because it was post-trained with modern Claude models as judge and conversation partner (verified, Talkie post and model card).

| Choice | Decision | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model | Base model for all confirmatory arms; chat model for the librarian mode and the demo; `talkie-web-13b-base` as a ceiling | The chat model carries modern-model influence; the web twin shares the architecture but knows the answers |
| Weights | Community transformers port in bf16 ([xlr8harder](https://huggingface.co/xlr8harder/talkie-1930-13b-base-tf)), checked against the official code's logits on a few dozen prompts before use | The official package has no log-probability call and returns last-position logits only (verified, [repo](https://github.com/talkie-lm/talkie)) |
| Quantisation | None | Porters report large weight outliers and broken fp8; it would save a few dollars and add a confound (reported) |
| Context | Prompts held under about 1,800 tokens until a context test says otherwise | The default was 2,048 at release and raised to 4,096 in May 2026; one porter reports quality falling past about 2k (reported) |
| Reading a forecast | Each question becomes a statement and its negation, three or four paraphrases each, placed under a period header such as a dated almanac chronicle. Probability is summed over paraphrases, turned into log-odds, and averaged over four to six templates | Different wordings of one answer split probability; a document-continuation frame suits a base model |
| Evidence | One retrieved passage per forward pass, pooled afterwards. A concatenated variant with random order is a secondary check | Removes order effects and context pressure on a weak model |
| Calibration | One temperature-and-bias map, cross-fitted over question families and fitted on all arms pooled | Calibration then cannot create a difference between arms |

Two checks run before any arm. A known-truth check: questions that resolved between 1900 and 1929 should score well closed book, which validates the reading method. A leak probe: every 1931 to 1940 question is asked closed book, since the authors say the 13B model knows some details of the Second World War and after (verified, Talkie post).

## Question set

The questions are generated by rule from lists fixed as of 1930, not picked by us, because hand-picked questions about the 1930s carry hindsight: we would ask about what happened. The full set is about 1,500 questions with a 150-question pilot. Composition and counts are a proposal.

### How questions are made

1. Fix the sampling frame before looking at outcomes: entity lists as of 1930 (sovereign states, the 48 States, the largest cities in the 1930 census, listed data series) crossed with templates and horizons.
2. Set numeric thresholds by rule from pre-1931 data, such as the median historical change over the same horizon. Never from 1930s values.
3. Extract contemporaries' own open questions mechanically from documents dated 1930 or earlier, and resolve all of them, including the dull ones.
4. Make every family exhaustive and mutually exclusive, or pair each item with an opposite. Target a yes rate of 40 to 60 per cent.
5. Write every question in language a 1930 reader would understand. No later vocabulary.
6. Train a simple bag-of-words classifier from question text to outcome. If it beats chance, the wording leaks and the family is rewritten.
7. Build the same families for 1921 to 1930 as an in-sample mirror of about 300 questions, unscored.
8. Check each family's wording before use (added 25 Nov 2026; the exact rule is a proposal). The reading method compares how likely a period writer was to write each statement, not how likely it was to be true, and some sentence shapes carry a habit of writing that outweighs what the model knows. In stage 0, comparisons between cities ("Albany had a larger population than Baltimore") scored below chance in two wordings, 0.245 and 0.128, in Talkie and in the web twin, while the same facts asked as top-10 membership scored 0.79: print over-represents surprising comparisons. Every family is therefore first asked in its exact wording about outcomes known by 1930 (1900 to 1929), and a family whose format scores at or below chance there is reworded or dropped. Comparative families, such as "Will Los Angeles outrank Detroit in 1940?", are the first to check.

The headline pairs are the famous events and are chosen with hindsight, so they are reported as their own stratum and never pooled. They are also the most likely to have leaked into Talkie's training data. Several of the datasets forbid redistribution, so the repo stores question text and outcomes derived from them and a fetch script, not the datasets.

### Leak checks

- Canaries: above-chance accuracy on unforecastable items means contamination.
- Tell-tale probes: the probability of strings such as "President Franklin D. Roosevelt" against matched decoys.
- The 1921 to 1930 mirror: out-of-sample skill close to in-sample skill is suspicious.
- A community probe set of post-1930 facts exists and can be reused (reported, `trumancai/talkie-1930-knowledge-bench`).
- Results are reported with and without flagged questions.

### Sample size

In a paired design the number of questions needed is about 7.85 times the variance of per-question score differences, divided by the squared effect (hypothesis, standard formula). With a standard deviation of 0.10, detecting a Brier improvement of 0.03 needs about 90 independent questions, 0.02 needs about 200 and 0.01 needs about 800. Questions cluster by election, country and series, so no cluster exceeds about 10 items and the pilot estimates the real variance and clustering before the full set is fixed. The 1930s are one draw of history with the Depression and the war as shared causes, so results are reported per family as well as pooled.

Talkie's own training corpus is not released (verified, Talkie repo), so the archive is assembled independently. Not used in the first build: Institutional Books and HathiTrust (gated, non-commercial, no redistribution), Trove (bulk saving of full text has led to cancelled API keys, reported), and British Library open newspapers (stop around 1896, verified). British press from 1900 to 1930 is the main gap.

### Rules for building it

- Hard date filter: publication date before 1931-01-01, taken from issue-level dates. Bound volumes that span years are excluded unless split by issue.
- Strip modern matter: derived fields such as topic tags and Wikidata links, library boilerplate, later prefaces and footnotes.
- Run an anachronism check over every passage and drop failures. Talkie's authors report that their own filter let through text about the New Deal and the war (verified, Talkie post).
- No re-OCR with modern vision models, which the authors found can insert modern facts (verified, Talkie post).
- Search is BM25 on CPU (SQLite FTS5 or Tantivy). No modern embedding model, since learned retrievers carry associations from after 1930 (verified, Paleka et al. 2025).
- Deduplicate wire copy and attribute a statement to its earliest dated appearance.
- Remove chat control markers from retrieved text before it reaches Talkie, since the official code would read them as control tokens (verified, Talkie repo).

First download for the pilot: American Stories 1925 to 1930, Newswire 1900 to 1930, Hansard 1919 to 1930, the Congressional Record for the 56th to 71st Congresses, and the Miller Center set. Rough size 50 to 80 GB (hypothesis; per-year sizes are not published).

## Reliability ledger

The ledger gives each source in a fixed panel a track record built from forecasts it made up to 1927 that had resolved by the end of 1930, with every verdict resting on a quoted pre-1931 passage. It is the part most exposed to hindsight, because a modern model builds it, and prompts cannot make a modern model forget: simulated ignorance left a 52 per cent gap against true ignorance in one study (verified, Li et al. 2026).

### Panel

- Sixty to 150 sources, chosen by a rule fixed in advance: sources with enough dated, forward-looking statements before 1928 and enough passages from 1928 to 1930 to appear as evidence. Newspapers by title, wire services, named columnists and forecasting services as echoed in newspapers, and named speakers in Parliament and Congress.
- Evidence shown to Talkie in the reliability arms comes only from panel sources, so every passage has a record.

### Protocol

1. Extract every forecast-like sentence from a deterministic sample of each source's passages, so the extractor cannot pick claims by what later happened.
2. Mask the source's identity before judging.
3. Require each verdict to quote a pre-1931 passage, and check in code that the quote exists in the archive. "Unresolved by 1930" is an allowed verdict.
4. Exclude claims whose resolution falls after 1930.
5. Plant canary claims that resolved only after 1930. Any verdict on them other than "unresolved" measures the leak rate.
6. Run Talkie as a second judge on a sample, have a person audit about 200 verdicts, and report agreement.
7. Score with shrinkage towards the mean of the source's type, since most sources will have few claims. Topic-specific scores only where a source has about ten or more claims on the topic.

### The gate: do track records persist?

Before any Talkie arm runs, score claims made up to 1920 on outcomes up to 1925, then test whether those scores predict the same source's accuracy from 1926 to 1930. If they do not, the ledger carries no signal and the experiment stops there with a finding. This gate needs no vintage model and is a backtest of reliability tracking in its own right. There is a reason to expect a weak result in one domain: Cowles's 1933 study found market forecasters did no better than chance (verified, [Cowles 1933](https://economics.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/cowles-forecasters33.pdf)).

### Seeds and period scorecards

- William Peter Hamilton's Wall Street Journal editorials, from the early 1900s to 1929 (the agents read the start year as 1903 and 1904): the one named forecaster in Cowles's study, with 90 position changes of which 45 were right (verified, Cowles 1933). The editorial texts are reported to be available from Yale (link verified, contents not opened).
- An appendix of dated Harvard Economic Society and Irving Fisher statements (verified link, [Dominguez, Fair and Shapiro 1988](https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/rayfair/pdf/1988C200.PDF)).
- Garfield Cox's 1929 appraisal of six business forecasting services, scored monthly for 1919 to 1929 (reported, [NBER](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c1086.pdf); not yet read). If it holds up, it is a track-record table written before the cutoff, with no modern judge involved, and could be shown to Talkie as it stands.
- Later studies are used only as pointers to pre-1931 text, never as evidence for a verdict.

Cost of building the ledger with a modern model is not yet estimated; the pilot on 20 sources will give a per-source figure. The existing engine runs at about $5 a subject (from the project's notes on the repo, 15 Oct), but that figure includes web search, which this build does not use.

## The librarian

The librarian is a modern, tool-capable model that makes tool calls on Talkie's behalf, so that the interactive mode tests Talkie's reasoning from its own knowledge and worldview and not its ability to format a tool call. It is Alex's proposal of 19 Nov. It runs in the exploratory mode only, because it knows how the 1930s went and is therefore a possible channel for hindsight. No prior work was found on a stronger model leaking hindsight to a weaker one through tool calls; the nearest results are that query expansion by a language model helps most where the generated text already contains the answer, and that instructions to forget do not work (verified, abstracts and Li et al. 2026).

### How a session runs

1. Talkie's chat model is given a question and told, in period terms, that it may write to a librarian who can search the newspapers and journals and look up how a source's past forecasts turned out.
2. Talkie writes a request in plain language.
3. The librarian turns the request into an archive search or a ledger lookup and returns the results.
4. Talkie may ask again, up to a fixed number of turns, then gives its forecast, which is read from token probabilities as in the fixed mode.

### Constraints, enforced in code where possible

- The librarian never sees the forecasting question, the arm or the outcome. It sees only Talkie's request.
- Its search terms may use only words that appear in Talkie's request. A check in code rejects any query containing other terms.
- Ranking is the fixed lexical search. The librarian does not choose among results.
- It returns verbatim passages at a fixed length, and ledger entries looked up by exact source key. No summaries, no commentary, no answers from its own knowledge.
- If Talkie asks about anything after 1930, or for the librarian's own view, the librarian replies with a fixed refusal line.
- Every exchange is logged. Any query that differs from a scripted parse of the same request is flagged for audit.

### Checks on the librarian

- Canary requests, such as "the German chancellor", confirm that no post-1930 term is added.
- A scripted run of the same sessions with no librarian shows how much the librarian itself changes the forecasts.
- A sample of sessions is read by a person before any transcript is used in a write-up or demo.

A community fine-tune of Talkie for tool calling exists (reported, `NyxKrage/talkie-1930-toolcalling`). It is an alternative to the librarian, but fine-tuning on modern tool-call data changes the model, so it is not used for the main result.

## Analysis plan

The primary result is the paired difference in Brier score between real and shuffled track records on the same questions and the same passages, tested separately for each of the three ways a track record enters. The plan is frozen and preregistered after the pilot and before the full run.

- **Metrics.** Brier score, log score, calibration curve and AUROC, reported together. AUROC is included because a score line of any kind may pull probabilities towards one half, which changes Brier without changing ranking.
- **Primary contrast.** Real against shuffled (H2), per mode A, B and C, with Holm correction across the three.
- **Secondary contrasts.** Real against none (H1), inverted against none (H3), and the dose check (H4): benefit against the spread of track records among the retrieved sources and their disagreement.
- **Tests.** Paired differences with a bootstrap clustered by question family, and a mixed model with random effects for question and family as a cross-check.
- **Ceiling for pooling.** An oracle run of mode C, weighting sources by their actual 1931 to 1940 accuracy, shows the most any track record could have delivered on this evidence.
- **Exclusions, fixed in advance.** Questions flagged by the leak probes are excluded from the primary analysis and reported separately. The headline stratum and the canaries are never pooled.
- **Reporting.** All arms and all modes are reported, including nulls. Results are given per question family as well as pooled.

### Three ways to get a false positive, and the control

| Risk | Control |
| --- | --- |
| Hindsight in the ledger: the modern judge favours sources it knows were later vindicated | Source-blind judging, quoted evidence checked in code, canary claims, the persistence gate, Talkie as second judge, a human audit |
| The placebo harms rather than the real records helping: Talkie already has views on named sources, and shuffled records conflict with them | Real must also beat no records; inverted must be worse than shuffled; the anonymous-source arm |
| A calibration or selection artefact | Identical cached passages across arms, one pooled calibration map, AUROC beside Brier, the dose check |

Preregistration template: Hofman et al. 2023, "Pre-registration for Predictive Modeling", is a candidate (reported, not checked).

## Stages, gates and cost

The work runs in five stages, each ending in a gate that can stop or reshape the experiment before more is spent. No dates are set; the first two stages are the cheap ones and decide whether the rest is worth doing.

| Stage | What is built | Gate to pass |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Model checks | Talkie served with log-probabilities; logit parity against the official code; context test at 1k, 2k, 3k and 4k tokens; leak probes on 1931 to 1940; the known-truth check on 1900 to 1929 | The reading method separates true from false on 1900 to 1929 questions, and the leak rate on the target decade is low enough to leave most questions usable |
| 1. Archive and ledger pilot | Pilot corpus indexed; ledger for about 20 sources; canary and audit results; the persistence test | Track records from before 1921 predict the same sources' accuracy in 1926 to 1930; ledger canaries show a low leak rate |
| 2. Pilot | 150 questions under all arms and modes; the synthetic task; the manipulation check | Mode A moves Talkie's output on the synthetic task; the pilot gives the variance and clustering needed to size the full set |
| 3. Full run | Preregistration frozen; full panel and ledger; about 1,500 questions; analysis and write-up | None: all results are reported |
| 4. Exploratory | Interactive librarian sessions; demo; replication at another cutoff | None |

A replication at a second cutoff is possible in stage 4: a 7B model with a 1913 cutoff is public (verified, `typewriter-ai/typewriter-1913-7B-base-v2`), which would allow the same design on the years of the First World War. The Zurich models with five cutoffs are not yet released (verified, their repo says upcoming).

### Compute

Recommended: [Modal](https://modal.com/pricing) with one 48 GB GPU (L40S, $1.95 an hour, billed per second, scales to zero), serving the bf16 transformers port behind a small scoring endpoint; search on CPU. The corpus and index live on a Modal volume, since the coding agent's cloud session has no GPU and limited disk. A RunPod pod is about 40 per cent cheaper per hour but bills until stopped.

## Risks

| Risk | Likelihood (hypothesis) | What limits it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Talkie already knows parts of the 1930s, most of all the famous events | High for the headline stratum, unknown elsewhere | Per-question leak probes, exclusion rule, rule-generated questions about less famous outcomes, paired design |
| Talkie is too weak to use evidence, so every arm sits near an uninformed score | Medium | Stage 0 known-truth check; one passage per pass; mode C does not need Talkie to read scores |
| Track records do not persist, so the ledger has no signal | Medium; Cowles found none for market forecasters | The stage 1 gate, which is a finding either way |
| Hindsight enters through the modern judge | Medium | The ledger protocol, canaries, audit, and the Cox 1929 table as a judge-free alternative |
| The ledger is too sparse: few sources with enough resolved claims | Medium | Panel chosen by a coverage rule; shrinkage; newspapers by title give volume |
| Results rest on unofficial ports of the model | Low to medium | Logit parity check against the official code |
| Dataset licences restrict redistribution | Certain for several | Repo stores derived questions and fetch scripts only |
| The 1930s are one draw of history | Certain | Per-family reporting; replication at the 1913 cutoff |
